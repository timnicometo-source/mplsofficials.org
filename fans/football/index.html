Friday Night Through Our Eyes

Officials watch more than the football

Every official has a specific area and set of players to watch. While one official follows the quarterback, others watch line play, receivers, sidelines, forward progress, the clock, and action away from the ball. If every official watched only the runner, much of the play would be missed.

Good to know

High school football is played under NFHS rules. Those rules are not always the same as college or NFL rules, even when the plays look identical.

The Five-Person Crew

Who watches what?

All five officials are not looking at the same spot. Each official has specific players and areas to watch before, during, and after the play. That means an official may not immediately know the result of the play because that official was watching something completely different.

R

White Hat

Referee

Offensive backfield

The referee's primary responsibility is the quarterback, including action around the passer and plays developing in the offensive backfield.

U

Interior Official

Umpire

Offensive backfield

The umpire keys the center, guard, and tackle on the left side of the offensive line, looking especially for holding, low blocks, and false starts.

DJ

Line of Scrimmage

Down Judge

On the line of scrimmage

The down judge watches for false starts and illegal formations, including five players in the offensive backfield. The down judge also rules on catches, incomplete passes, and forward progress near that sideline.

LJ

Line of Scrimmage

Line Judge

Opposite side of the line of scrimmage

The line judge has responsibilities similar to the down judge: watching the line and formation, ruling on catches and incomplete passes, and marking forward progress on that side of the field.

BJ

Deep Official

Back Judge

Deep in the defensive backfield

The back judge's keys change with the offensive formation. The back judge watches designated receivers and rules on catches, incomplete passes, pass interference, and most touchdowns scored on pass plays.

Why didn't that official see it?

The nearest official is not always responsible for the action everyone in the stands is watching. Good crew coverage depends on each official staying with an assigned key instead of following the football.

Subvarsity and Youth Football

Fewer officials, more field to cover

Many local subvarsity and youth games use three- or four-person crews rather than the five-person crew described above. These smaller crews normally have no dedicated back judge. Staffing and assignments can vary by league.

Safety comes first

Officials prioritize dangerous contact, including illegal helmet contact, late hits, and other personal fouls. Protecting players remains a priority before, during, and after every play.

Get the play started fairly

Before the snap, officials watch for false starts, encroachment, and other formation or substitution problems. Catching these fouls helps keep the game orderly and prevents an unfair start.

Shared downfield coverage

Without a dedicated back judge, the remaining officials share more receiver, pass, goal-line, and sideline coverage. Each official must watch more players, and some actions may be screened from view.

The rules still apply to everyone.

A smaller crew does not give players permission to hold or interfere with an opponent. It means fewer viewing angles and more responsibility for each official. Safety fouls and clear pre-snap fouls remain priorities while the crew works to cover the entire play.

One of Football's Most Misunderstood Fouls

What makes it holding?

A jersey grab or an arm outside the opponent's body can draw a shout from the stands, but officials need to see what the contact actually does. Here is a useful way to understand how officials evaluate potential offensive holding during a block.

01

Where is the point of attack?

The point of attack is the area where the play is developing and where a block can affect an opponent's opportunity to participate. It is an area, not a painted spot on the field.

On a run, it includes the running lane and defenders who can reach the runner. On a pass, it includes protection around the passer and a rusher's route to the quarterback. Officials consider whether the restriction affects that action.

02

Was there material restriction?

Material restriction means the illegal contact meaningfully limits the opponent's movement or ability to participate. The player may be pulled off balance, turned, restrained, dragged down, or forced away from an intended path.

Contact alone is not enough. Officials watch the whole engagement: did the opponent move freely through the contact, or did the blocker actually prevent or delay that movement?

03

Which holding “bucket” fits?

A bucket is an officiating shorthand for a recognizable category of illegal action. It helps an official identify what happened instead of throwing a flag simply because a block looked awkward.

Three useful starting categories are takedown, tackle, and hook and restrict. Other recognizable forms appear below.

These are judgment tools, not a blanket exception to the rules.

Point of attack is an officiating philosophy, not a separate NFHS rulebook definition. It helps officials evaluate a block's effect on the play. Obvious takedowns, dangerous acts, and personal fouls still require attention even away from the ball. Defensive holding also has its own applications.

Three primary holding categories

Officials look for an illegal technique that actually restricts the opponent.

Takedown

The blocker uses an illegal grab, twist, or pull to bring the opponent to the ground. A player falling during a legal block is not automatically evidence of holding.

Tackle

A player acting as a blocker wraps up or tackles an opponent instead of legally blocking. Tackling the ball carrier is part of football; tackling an opponent to keep that opponent out of the play is different.

Hook and restrict

The blocker hooks an arm around the opponent and limits the opponent's movement. For example, an arm across a rusher's body may force the rusher to take a longer route to the quarterback. An extended arm without restriction is not automatically holding.

Other forms officials recognize

Grab and restrict

A player grabs the opponent or jersey and uses that grip to prevent separation, slow pursuit, or turn the opponent away from the play.

Jerk and restrict

A blocker grips the opponent and abruptly pulls or redirects the opponent, delaying or changing the opponent's path.

Pullover

The blocker pulls the opponent down over the blocker's own body. This can make it appear that the opponent simply ran over the blocker, so officials need to see how the action began.

What should fans look for?

Watch the opponent's movement, not just the blocker's hands. Did an illegal action stop, turn, or pull the player away from a chance to participate? A strong legal block can put a player on the ground. A visible jersey stretch can help an official see a possible hold, but the complete action matters.

Common High School Rule Questions

Friday football has its own rules

Many familiar college and professional rules work differently in a high school game. These are a few of the differences spectators notice most often.

01

The runner is down without contact

In high school, a runner is down when a knee or another body part other than a hand or foot touches the ground. A defender does not need to make contact.

02

A kick in the end zone is dead

When a free kick or scrimmage kick breaks the plane of the receiving team's goal line, it is a touchback and cannot be returned. The ball is placed at the 20-yard line.

03

The defense cannot score on a try

If the defense gains possession during a high school try, the ball becomes dead and the defense cannot return it for points.

04

Only four fouls carry an automatic first down

An easy memory aid is that all four begin with “R”: roughing the passer, roughing the snapper, roughing the kicker, and roughing the holder.

05

Forward progress determines the spot

The ball is spotted where the runner's forward advance ends—not necessarily where the runner finally falls. If defenders stop the runner and drive the runner backward, the ball returns to the forward-progress spot. Officials may allow a brief moment for the runner to keep advancing before declaring the play dead, but once forward progress is ruled stopped, the play is over.

Minnesota Timing Rule

How the mercy rule works

When the point differential reaches 35 points or more in the fourth quarter, the game clock changes to running time.

35+

Running time begins

The rule applies only in the fourth quarter. If the point differential later drops below 30 points, regular timing rules resume.
