Seeing the Whole Court

A plain-language look at what basketball officials watch, how crews divide the court, and why familiar calls are often more complicated than they appear.

More Than Following the Ball

Each official has a different view

Basketball moves quickly, and the crew cannot all watch the player with the ball. One official may watch the defender guarding the dribbler while another watches players cutting, screening, rebounding, or competing away from the ball.

Good to know

Officials regularly change positions as possession and direction change. Responsibility belongs to the official with the best assigned angle—not automatically the one nearest the play.

Two- and Three-Person Crews

Who watches what?

Position names describe where an official is located during a possession. The crew rotates and switches positions throughout the game.

L

End Line Position

Lead

Along the end line

Primarily watches post play, drives toward the basket, rebounding contact, and action near the lane and baseline.

T

Backcourt Position

Trail

Behind the play

Watches the ball and defender in much of the perimeter, helps with rebounding and shooting action, and observes players away from the lead.

C

Opposite-Side Position

Center

Opposite the Lead Official

Used in a three-person crew to provide an additional angle on drives, off-ball matchups, screens, and rebounding action.

Two Officials, More Court to Cover

Why do some games have only two officials?

Most varsity high school basketball games use three officials. Subvarsity and youth games usually use two, although staffing can vary by league and level. With two officials, each must cover more players and more of the court.

During a shot, one official stays with the shooter to watch for illegal contact through the landing. The other official may be watching rebounding and off-ball contact involving seven or eight players. With fewer viewing angles, some contact can be screened from both officials.

Protecting the shooter remains a priority. An official who turns toward the rebound too early could miss contact that affects the shot or the shooter’s safe landing. Rebounding fouls still matter, but a two-person crew must divide its attention across more action.

Why are two officials looking away from the ball?

Some of basketball’s most important action happens before a pass arrives: screens, cuts, holding, displacement, and players establishing legal guarding position.

Calls Fans Ask About

What did the official see?

01

Contact is not automatically a foul

Basketball includes contact. Officials judge whether a player was placed at a disadvantage through illegal contact, not simply whether bodies touched.

02

Traveling is about the pivot foot

The number of steps alone does not tell the whole story. Officials determine when control was gained, which foot became the pivot foot, and what happened next.

03

A defender does not have to be motionless

After establishing legal guarding position, a defender may move laterally or backward. The ruling depends on position and responsibility for the contact.

04

Possession arrows alternate

Many held-ball situations use the alternating-possession arrow rather than a jump ball. The arrow changes after the throw-in ends.

05

Officials may wait before signaling

On some plays, an official briefly observes whether contact affects the shot, pass, dribble, or player before deciding whether a foul occurred.

06

High school is not the NBA

Professional rules for continuation, defensive restrictions, court markings, timing, and replay should not be assumed during a high school game.

Common Misunderstandings

“That’s Not a Rule”

Familiar phrases do not always describe the high school rule. Here is what officials actually judge.

From the stands

“Over the back!”

Reaching above another player for a rebound is legal. Pushing, holding, or displacing that player can be a foul; height alone is not.

From the stands

“That’s a reach-in!”

Reaching toward the ball is legal. Illegal contact, such as holding or hitting an opponent’s arm, can make the play a foul.

From the stands

“The defender was moving!”

A defender must first establish legal guarding position, initially facing the opponent with both feet on the court. The defender can then move laterally or backward; being motionless is not required.

From the stands

“You can’t rebound your own air ball!”

Under high school rules, a player may recover their own missed shot if the official judges it a genuine try for a basket.

From the stands

“The dribble went above the shoulder!”

A high bounce alone is legal. The issue is whether the ball comes to rest in the hand or the player otherwise ends the dribble.

From the stands

“It hit a foot—kick!”

Accidental contact with a foot or leg is not a kicking violation. Intentionally using any part of the leg to strike the ball is.

From the stands

“Three seconds—even during the rebound!”

There is no three-second count after a shot is released and before a rebound is controlled. A new count requires the applicable team-control conditions.

From the stands

“The pivot foot came up—travel!”

Lifting the pivot foot for a pass or shot can be legal if the ball is released before that foot returns. Starting a dribble has different requirements.

These are short explanations for fans and spectators, not every exception. They describe NFHS high school play; college, professional, and local youth rules can differ. NFHS basketball rules resources
